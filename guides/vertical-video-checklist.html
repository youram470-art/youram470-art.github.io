A vertical video checklist for AI-generated footage

26 November 2026 · production notes

Most vertical video problems are decided before anything is generated. The frame is chosen, the subject is placed, and then a horizontal composition is cropped into a tall one. The crop works often enough to be tempting, and fails badly on exactly the shots that carry the video: faces, products, and anything the viewer has to read.

This checklist is the version I use now. It runs in order, because each step constrains the next, and doing them out of order is how a shot ends up needing a re-render at the last minute.

1. Decide the axis before the subject

A wide frame uses left and right for context. A tall frame uses top and bottom for scale and depth: ceilings, sky, floors, reflections. If the scene has nothing above and nothing below, it will feel cramped no matter where the subject sits. Before describing the subject, describe what fills the top third and the bottom third.

2. Move back and lower

The single most reliable fix for a cramped vertical shot is a wider, slightly lower camera. It buys headroom above the subject and creates a foreground plane below, which reads as depth in a tall frame. Ten percent more distance and twenty centimetres lower fixes more vertical portraits than any lighting change, and the same adjustment translates directly into a prompt.

3. Reserve the caption band

Vertical delivery almost always includes burned-in text. Decide where it goes before generating, because a frame composed for a caption band is composed differently: the subject sits higher, the lower fifth stays uncluttered, and the background there is quiet enough for text to sit on. A band added afterwards either covers the subject or needs a scrim, and a scrim is the fastest way to make a shot look like an ad.

4. Match the move to the frame

Tilts and rises feel native in a tall frame; pans feel native in a wide one. Using a horizontal pan in a vertical crop leaves dead space at the edges of the move and crops the subject for most of the travel. If the shot needs lateral movement, either widen the subject's path or change the move. The framing rules that make this automatic when prompting are set out in the Wan 3 vertical framing notes, which describe tall compositions in the prompt rather than cropping after the fact.

5. Keep one element moving

Generated stills often look like stills because everything is static. One moving element — hair, fabric, steam, a reflection, a passing shadow — is enough to make the frame read as a moment rather than a photograph. Two or more competing movements make a short clip busy, and busy reads as artificial in a small frame.

6. Review at phone size, muted, in one hand

This is the only review that matches how the video will be seen. At phone size, mild softness and compression artefacts disappear, and structural errors stay. Muted, the video has to work on composition and movement alone. One hand, the frame is partly covered by a thumb, which is exactly the condition a caption band is protecting against.

7. Check the first second and the last second

The first second decides whether the video is watched, and generated clips usually have a wind-up that should be trimmed. The last second decides whether it is remembered, and generated sequences usually just stop. A hold, a resolving move, or a cut to black with sound carrying through all read as intentional. The difference between a clip that stops and a clip that ends is usually one second of material.

8. Write the shot down before generating it

The checklist only works if the shot exists in words first: axis, distance, height, move, motion cue, caption band. Six lines per shot. It takes a minute and removes most of the re-render cycles, because the failures above are visible in the description before they are visible in the render.
